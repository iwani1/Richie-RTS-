Daylight 2 — the same product, with its edges sharpened

You said the interface looks plain. I agree, and I do not think the answer is a new colour scheme — the diagnostics say the app already has a consistent palette and one accent. What it lacks is typographic confidence and hierarchy: everything sits in a white box at the same weight, at 12.5 pixel text, so the eye has nowhere to land and the screen reads as grey.

This page shows the same screens twice — as they are, and as I propose they become. Read it, then tell me which sections to keep and which to throw out. Nothing in the application has been changed to produce it.

What changed here, and why. The eyebrow above the title repeated the breadcrumb directly above it, so it went; the paragraph moved into one line of real figures. Four shadowed cards became one strip with hairline dividers — same four numbers, a third of the vertical space, and nothing floating. The table lost its zebra-free-but-flat feel: an uppercase micro-label header, ledger-aligned figures, and status chips that carry a dot and a word.

What changed here. Labels are sentence case and read at 12 px in ink rather than uppercase grey — uppercase micro-labels are kept for table headers and section counts, where they help you scan, and taken off form labels, where they slow reading. The error names the consequence, not just the rule. The primary action is the only filled control, everything else is quiet.

03The whole workspace

The frame matters as much as the page inside it. The rail is tinted and the work surface is light: the chrome recedes, the records come forward. The active destination is a white chip with a 2 pixel indigo edge, and its outstanding count sits on the right of the row where a scan finds it.

Why a detail screen and not another list. This is the shape the registers open into: one record, the thing that needs a decision on the left, the facts you would otherwise scroll to find pinned on the right. It is also where the newest piece of work lands — the charges added after the price was agreed, each with the name that signed it.

04What actually changes

  1. Body text goes from 12.5 to 13.5–14 pixels, and greys get darker.

    Small grey text at low contrast is the single biggest cause of “this looks cheap”. The muted ink moves from #667085 (4.97:1) to #59637a (6.02:1), and the ink itself to near-black #0b1220 (18.7:1). Everything stays AA; most of it moves to AAA.

  2. Titles get bigger and tighter; labels get smaller and quieter.

    Page titles at 26 px with −0.026em tracking, figures at 25 px with tabular numerals, table headers at 9.5 px uppercase. A real jump between three sizes reads as designed; three sizes one pixel apart reads as flat.

  3. Fewer boxes, more rule and space.

    Four floating stat cards become one bordered strip with hairlines. Cards lose their shadow and keep a 1 px edge; the shadow is reserved for things that genuinely float above the page, like a dialog. Nothing on the canvas competes for depth.

  4. Numbers are read down a column, not across a line.

    Every amount, date and count is right-aligned with tabular numerals, so a glance down the total column compares like with like. Currency sits in a small mono label rather than inside every figure.

  5. Status becomes a chip with a dot and a word, not a rounded pill.

    Fully rounded pills read as 2020. A 6 px radius with a 5 px dot is quieter and scans faster, and the word is still there — colour is never the only carrier of meaning.

  6. The chrome recedes: tinted rail, light canvas, white records.

    Today the rail and the work surface are the same white and the cards float on top. Inverting that relationship gives the page a frame and makes the records the brightest thing on screen.

  7. One place for the explanation, on demand.

    Long “why this figure” paragraphs move behind a quiet link next to the figure they explain. The sentence is still there, in full, for the person who needs it — it simply stops being the first thing every reader meets.

  8. The same treatment everywhere, in one pass.

    The tokens and the six components are already shared, so this is a pass over the 30 screens, not a rewrite: 485 leftover colour classes removed, sorting on every register, one header pattern, one empty state. That is what makes it read as one product rather than thirty.

05The rules this keeps

  • One accent, unchanged. #4453f0 is still the only action colour, at 5.6:1 on white.
  • No gradients, glass, 3D or shadow tricks. Flat tints, one edge, one hairline.
  • No motion. Nothing animates, nothing fades in; transitions stay off.
  • No colour-only meaning. Every chip and notice carries its words; the dot is a second cue, not the cue.
  • No new dependency. The same Inter and IBM Plex Mono files already in the app, the same CSS.
  • Nothing invented. Empty states still say what is missing rather than showing a zero.

06How it would land

Three steps, each one shippable on its own, so you can stop after any of them.

  1. Step one — the tokens and the six components.

    Colour, type scale, radii, row heights and the button/field/chip/notice/empty-state/stat strip set in src/index.css and src/ui/primitives.tsx. Every screen improves at once because they all draw from those files; nothing is redesigned screen by screen yet.

  2. Step two — the frame and the three busiest screens.

    The rail, the top bar, then dashboard, rentals and invoices. This is the step you would show somebody.

  3. Step three — the sweep.

    The remaining registers onto the shared table with sorting, the leftover colour classes gone, and every screen's empty state and error message brought to one voice. Existing tests should keep passing; where a test asserts old wording I will change the test and say so.
