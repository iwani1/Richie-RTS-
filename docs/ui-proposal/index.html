A board, not a stack of boxes

You were right that the last version was still boring, and Dribbble shows exactly what it was missing: rhythm. A premium screen is not uniform. Cards are different sizes and hold different kinds of thing — a figure with a trend, a list of people, a bar of days, a table — and they fit together like a board. Uniform white rectangles in a row will always read as a form, however carefully they are styled.

So the dashboard below is a bento board built from the same pieces as before: a large “today” card with the next departures, figures with a delta and a sparkline, a fleet bar that splits 15/4/3, the register, the charges waiting for a second name, papers about to lapse, the week's money, and who is on the road. Every card is a real screen of this product — nothing invented to fill a grid.

Three skins of the same board. Pick one, or name a mixture.

01Three finishes of one board

Each panel holds two screens: the board — the dashboard, where the bento rhythm does the work — and the register — a list screen, where the same treatment has to stay calm. The rows are illustrations for this page; they are not records and they are not in the database.

What makes these read as premium rather than just tidy. A hairline edge plus a soft, wide, very low-opacity shadow gives a card weight without a heavy outline. The primary button carries a 1px inner highlight and a coloured shadow, so it sits above the surface. Figures are 28px with tight tracking and tabular numerals: two columns of money line up digit for digit. Section labels are 9.5px uppercase with wide letter-spacing; page titles are 30px. Nothing sits at 12.5px midway between them any more — that single change is most of the difference between “flat” and “designed”.

02What was taken from Dribbble, and what was refused

  1. Unequal cards on a 12-column grid.

    A 5-3-4 top row, then 8-4, then 4-4-4. The eye gets a path instead of a queue, and the important card can simply be the biggest one.

  2. A figure, a delta and a sparkline in one card.

    34px number, a small pill saying what changed, and the seven-day shape underneath. It is the single device that makes a dashboard look alive rather than printed.

  3. People are shown as people.

    Initials in a tinted circle wherever a driver, a client or an author is the subject — in the charge list, the expiring papers, who is on the road. A table of names is a spreadsheet; a list with faces is an operations desk.

  4. Every card header carries its own action.

    “Run sheet”, “Readiness”, “Dispatch”, “All 6” — a quiet button at the top right of the card that goes where the card came from. Nothing on the board is a dead end.

  1. Refused: the rainbow of stat cards.

    Much of Dribbble's admin work paints each figure pink, blue, yellow and green. It photographs beautifully and destroys meaning: if every card is a colour, colour stops telling you anything. Status keeps its tints; actions keep the one indigo.

  2. Refused: gradient fills on charts.

    A soft fade under a line is a gradient. Here the area under the sparkline is one flat tint, and the weekly bars are flat — no fade, no glow.

  3. Refused: motion and glass.

    No counting numbers, no cards lifting on hover, no frosted panels. Depth comes from a hairline edge and a wide, quiet shadow, and nowhere else.

  4. Refused: invented content to fill a grid.

    No fake charts of “revenue vs target”. Every card is a screen that exists in this product, and where the records cannot answer, the card says so in words.

03What I would build, and in what order

  1. Pick a skin, then the tokens.

    Colour, radius, shadow and type scale go into src/index.css as variables. Every one of the 30 screens draws from that file, so all of them step up at once — no screen-by-screen redesign for the first visible win.

  2. Rebuild the six pieces in src/ui/primitives.tsx.

    Button, field, chip, notice, empty state and the figure strip. Fixing these six fixes every form, every register and every dialog in the application, because they are all made of them.

  3. The frame, then the board and the two busiest registers.

    Rail, top bar, then the dashboard rebuilt in the bento rhythm above, then rentals and invoices. This is the step that changes how the product feels on opening it.

  4. The sweep.

    The remaining registers onto the shared table with sorting, the 485 leftover colour classes removed, one header pattern and one empty state everywhere. Tests keep passing; where a test asserts wording I change, I will say so.

05The rules none of this breaks

  1. One accent.

    Indigo #4453f0 remains the only action colour. Studio lightens it to #7c88ff only because the same indigo fails on a dark surface; buttons stay on the original.

  2. No gradients at all.

    Depth comes from a hairline edge, a 1px inner highlight on the top of a card and a wide low-opacity shadow. The brand tile is solid indigo with a specular top edge, not a gradient.

  3. No motion.

    Nothing animates. A shadow can suggest depth; it does not have to move to do it.

  1. Colour never carries meaning alone.

    Every chip has a dot and a word; every notice has its sentence. The skin changes, the vocabulary does not.

  2. No new dependency, no new font.

    Inter and IBM Plex Mono are already bundled with the application. Nothing is installed for this page either.

  3. Nothing invented.

    Sample rows are labelled as illustrations here. In the application an unknown figure still says “not recorded” rather than showing a zero.
